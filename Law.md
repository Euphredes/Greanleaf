Yes. And I think the useful form for your project is **not a dump of millions of statutes**, but a compressed operational representation of American governance: a set of *do's and don'ts* extracted from the legal system.

There is an important scope problem, though. There is no finite document called "all American laws." The United States has federal, state, territorial, tribal, county, municipal, and administrative law. Federal law alone is preserved historically in the *Statutes at Large*, while the current general and permanent federal statutes are consolidated into the U.S. Code. ([Library of Congress][1]) The U.S. Code currently has 54 titles plus five appendices, and it deliberately excludes regulations, court decisions, treaties, and state/local law. 

So for **gambit**, I'd build what I would call:

# THE AMERICAN GOVERNANCE RULE SET

**Purpose:** represent the practical permissions, prohibitions, duties, powers, procedures, and consequences imposed by American government upon a person living within its jurisdiction.

The fundamental distinction would be:

> **LAW DOES NOT TELL THE SOVEREIGN WHAT TO BELIEVE.
> LAW TELLS THE PERSON WHAT GOVERNMENT MAY DO, WHAT THE PERSON MAY DO, WHAT THE PERSON MUST DO, AND WHAT HAPPENS WHEN THE TWO COLLIDE.**

That maps unusually well onto your project.

---

## I. GOVERNMENT MAY

Government possesses legally delegated powers.

### 1. Tax

Government may impose and collect taxes pursuant to lawful authority.

**DO:** report taxable activity when required.
**DON'T:** evade taxes.

### 2. Regulate

Government may regulate conduct within its lawful jurisdiction.

**DO:** comply with applicable regulation.
**DON'T:** assume that personal sovereignty nullifies regulation.

### 3. Criminalize

Government may define certain conduct as criminal and punish violations through lawful process.

**DO:** refrain from prohibited conduct.
**DON'T:** commit acts defined as crimes.

### 4. License and permit

Government may condition certain activities upon licenses, registrations, permits, inspections, etc.

**DO:** obtain required authorization.
**DON'T:** perform regulated activity without it.

### 5. Inspect and enforce

Government may investigate and enforce laws subject to constitutional and statutory limits.

**DO:** understand the authority being exercised.
**DON'T:** obstruct lawful enforcement.

### 6. Adjudicate

Government may resolve disputes through courts and administrative tribunals.

**DO:** use available legal procedures.
**DON'T:** substitute personal judgment for a court's jurisdiction merely because you disagree with its decision.

---

# II. GOVERNMENT MAY NOT

This is equally important to your constitutional project.

Government is itself constrained.

### 7. Government is not sovereign without limit

The Constitution establishes governmental powers and limitations.

**DON'T:** assume government possesses every power merely because it possesses physical force.

### 8. Government may not simply erase constitutional rights

Speech, religion, assembly, petition, due process, equal protection, protections against unreasonable searches, etc., create constraints upon government.

### 9. Government must follow legally prescribed procedure

The government generally cannot simply say:

> "because we said so."

It must have authority and follow applicable procedure.

That is potentially one of the strongest parallels with your **arbitrary / objecticality** distinction.

---

# III. THE PERSON MAY

The American system is not simply a list of government prohibitions.

There is a huge category of conduct that government leaves to the individual.

### 10. Speak

**DO:** express opinions, subject to applicable law.

### 11. Associate

**DO:** form relationships, organizations, businesses, communities, etc.

### 12. Assemble

**DO:** gather lawfully.

### 13. Petition

**DO:** challenge government.

### 14. Travel

**DO:** move through the country subject to lawful restrictions.

### 15. Contract

**DO:** voluntarily enter enforceable agreements.

### 16. Own property

**DO:** acquire, possess, use, transfer, and dispose of property subject to law.

### 17. Work

**DO:** engage in lawful employment or enterprise.

### 18. Refuse

A person generally retains enormous discretion over what they **do not** voluntarily undertake, unless law imposes a duty.

This distinction matters enormously for your sovereign-in-sovereignty concept.

---

# IV. THE PERSON MUST

This is where sovereignty encounters obligation.

### 19. Obey valid law

**DO:** comply with laws applicable to you.

### 20. Pay legally imposed taxes

**DO:** satisfy legally imposed tax obligations.

### 21. Honor enforceable contracts

**DO:** perform contractual obligations or accept legally prescribed consequences.

### 22. Comply with lawful court orders

**DO:** obey orders issued within lawful jurisdiction unless successfully challenged or stayed.

### 23. Comply with compulsory governmental processes

Depending upon circumstances, this can include things such as jury service, subpoenas, registration requirements, reporting obligations, etc.

### 24. Respect other people's rights

Your sovereignty terminates where your conduct becomes legally actionable against another person.

---

# V. GOVERNMENT MUST

This is the other half of the constitution.

### 25. Have legal authority

Government action requires an appropriate source of authority.

### 26. Follow procedure

Government generally must use the procedures prescribed by law.

### 27. Provide due process where required

A person cannot simply be deprived of protected interests without whatever process the Constitution or applicable law requires.

### 28. Respect constitutional limitations

The government is subordinate to the constitutional order.

### 29. Provide legally prescribed remedies

Where the law creates a right, it generally also creates some mechanism for enforcement or remedy.

Yes. If we translate the American governance rule set into operations-research language, I would make one fundamental distinction first:

> **Constraints describe the feasible region. Objectives describe what the sovereign is trying to optimize within that feasible region.**

So "government may" is generally **not an objective for the individual**. It is a constraint on the individual's feasible operating space: government has a permitted action set, and the sovereign must account for it.

Likewise, "government may not" is a constraint on the government's action space.

Your overall system is therefore not a single LP. It is best represented as a **mixed-integer nonlinear program (MINLP)**, because American governance contains:

* continuous quantities: money, time, distance, resources;
* discrete decisions: comply/not comply, license/no license, jurisdiction A/B;
* logical conditions: if X, then Y;
* thresholds;
* nonlinear consequences;
* multiple interacting jurisdictions.

Suppose the individual has decision variables:

[

and American governance supplies:

[

]

with:

[

* jurisdiction-dependent,
* mutually exclusive,

* (x) = conduct,
* (z) = jurisdictional/discrete state,

## And this gives me my answer to your actual question

If you force me to choose a decision-making classification:

> **America's law is an overdetermined mixed-integer constraint system, but not inherently an infeasible one.**

Its apparent impossibility arises if you commit the modeling error of treating **every law ever enacted as simultaneously active, universally applicable, and hard**.

The better formulation is:

[
\boxed{
\text{law corpus}
\rightarrow
\text{jurisdiction}
\rightarrow
\text{facts}
\rightarrow
\text{active constraints}
\rightarrow
\text{feasible region}
}
]

Then the sovereign solves:

[
\boxed{
\max S(x)
\quad
\text{subject to the active legal constraints}
}
]

And *this* is where your "conduct precedes the system" proposition becomes extremely interesting.

**Conduct generates the factual state (a).**

The factual state determines which laws activate.

Those laws generate the feasible region.

The sovereign then chooses conduct inside that region.

So the system is recursive:

[
\boxed{
\text{conduct}
\rightarrow
\text{facts}
\rightarrow
\text{law}
\rightarrow
\text{constraints}
\rightarrow
\text{feasible conduct}
\rightarrow
\text{new conduct}
}
]

That, rather than simply calling American law "overdetermined," is the model I would choose before you begin your own analysis.
